Models, maturity and hardware

This is the page for the awkward questions. What actually runs well on device today? What will my laptop do? Can I sell what comes out of it? Short answers, in that order.

If you only read one section, read maturity by modality — it is the difference between a good afternoon and a frustrating one.

Two runtimes under eight panels

SeasonStudio does not invent its own inference engine. It leans on two well-travelled local stacks, and which one a panel uses determines what file format you feed it and which backends are available.

Panel Runtime Model format
Text, Translate llama.cpp / GGML .gguf, usually 4-bit quantised
Music, Text to speech, Speech to text GGML .gguf
Image stable-diffusion.cpp / ONNX Runtime Model file plus a matching VAE
Video ONNX Runtime Model files plus a shared text encoder
Vision ONNX Runtime .onnx

Backends

Each runtime enumerates what your machine offers and takes the fastest option it can use. CPU always works and is always available; the accelerated backends are part of the one-time Premium unlock.

Platform GGML backends ONNX Runtime providers
Windows 10 / 11, x64 CPU · Vulkan · CUDA CPU · DirectML · CUDA
macOS, Apple silicon CPU · Metal CPU · CoreML

CUDA is the fast path on NVIDIA hardware; DirectML covers AMD, Intel and NVIDIA alike on Windows; Metal covers Apple silicon. On a machine with none of them the app still runs — it simply asks for more patience.

Maturity by modality

Open, locally runnable models did not all arrive at the same time or at the same quality. Text and image generation have had years of iteration; on-device music and video are much younger. Here is where we think each panel genuinely stands, and why.

Ready Text and translation

Quantised instruct models in the 1‑to‑8‑billion parameter range are the most settled part of local AI. They draft, summarise and restructure reliably at paragraph and page scale. Dedicated translation models go further still: for major language pairs they hold register and terminology better than a general model of similar size. The honest limits are knowledge and reasoning depth — a small local model does not know recent events, and will produce a confident wrong answer rather than admit uncertainty.

Ready Image generation

This is the modality where local and hosted quality have converged most. The SDXL and SD 3.5 lines, and the newer families alongside them, produce illustration and concept work you can put in front of a client. Prompt adherence has improved substantially, editing models let you change one thing without redrawing everything, and the sampler preview means a bad seed costs seconds rather than minutes. What remains hard: legible text inside the image, hands and crowds, and exact compositional control.

Ready Speech, both directions

Transcription is a solved problem for clean audio, and it is the task where running locally is most obviously worth it — recordings are the most sensitive thing most people feed a model. Synthesis has caught up quickly: current small speech models are natural enough for narration, and describing a voice in words rather than picking from a list genuinely works. The seams show over long passages, where prosody loses the thread, so work paragraph by paragraph.

Ready Vision

The vision stack is made of small, mature, purpose-built models rather than one ambitious one, which is exactly why it is dependable. Captioning, OCR, classification, detection and segmentation all behave predictably on ordinary photographs and load in a second. Accuracy falls off with handwriting, stylised type and heavy compression, and detection can only name classes it was trained on.

Early Music and sound

The Stable Audio family made short-form audio generation practical on a desktop, and within its range the results are genuinely usable: effects, textures, ambient beds and loops. Beyond that range the field simply has not arrived. Long-form musical structure — a theme that develops, a chorus that returns — is not something the current generation of open models does, and vocals are out of scope. Our advice is to use it for the layer underneath rather than the tune on top.

Experimental Video

Local video generation is the most exciting and least dependable thing in the app. The open text-to-video and image-to-video models can produce a few seconds of coherent motion, and occasionally something lovely, but the cost is high: gigabytes of model files, minutes per render, modest resolution, and identity that drifts across frames. We ship it because it is real and improving fast, and we label it experimental because we would rather set your expectations than sell you a surprise.

Why say this at all?

Because the alternative is you discovering it on a deadline. The gap between modalities is the single most useful thing to know about on-device creative AI, and it is the thing marketing pages usually leave out.

Hardware expectations

These figures are estimates

The tiers below are our own expectations from development machines, not results from a broad measurement pass. Treat them as a guide to which column you are in, not as a benchmark. They will be replaced with measured numbers.

Find your machine in one of the three tiers, then read across. The point of the table is not exact timings — it is knowing which panels to open first.

Tier A machine like this What to expect
Light Thin laptop, 8 GB RAM, integrated graphics or a base Apple silicon machine. Text, Translate, Speech to text, Text to speech and Vision are all comfortable. Image works at 512×512 with a light model — expect tens of seconds per picture. Music is slow but usable for short clips. Skip Video.
Comfortable 16 GB RAM with an 8 GB graphics card, or an Apple silicon Pro machine with 16 GB or more unified memory. The sweet spot. Image at 1024×1024 in well under a minute, larger language and translation models, longer transcriptions, Music without waiting around. Video becomes possible for very short clips at low resolution.
Roomy 32 GB RAM with a 16 GB or larger graphics card, or an Apple silicon Max machine. Everything, including Video at the resolutions the models were actually designed for. Heavy image models, batches of variations and the largest language models all fit.

Two things that matter more than the tier

  • Video memory, not system memory. Image and video models want to sit on the graphics card. A machine with plenty of RAM but 4 GB of video memory will behave like the Light tier for those two panels, however fast its processor is.
  • Disk space. Models are large files. A serious image shelf is tens of gigabytes; adding video pushes that well past a hundred. The studio only fetches what a run needs, which keeps this honest, but plan the space.

What can go on the shelf

One compact multimodal language model is built into the app, so the Text panel and the content safety gates work out of the box with nothing downloaded. Every other model is yours to add — either fetched on demand when a run needs it, or imported from a folder you already have.

Panel Families it can load Rough size on disk
Text Compact instruct models, and larger ones in the 4‑to‑8‑billion class 0.5 – 6 GB
Translate Dedicated translation models, small and large 1 – 5 GB
Image SD 1.5, SDXL, SD 3.5, newer open families, and editing models — each with a VAE 2 – 20 GB each

The exact list of models the app offers moves as the field does. The guide explains how to add one you already have.

Licences, and whether you can sell the output

SeasonStudio is a player, not a publisher. The app's own licence covers the app; every model you load stays under the licence its authors chose, and that licence — not ours — decides what you may do with what comes out.

The one thing to check before commercial work

Open model licences vary more than people expect: some are permissive, some are community licences with conditions, and a few are research-only. The app lists the licence next to every model it can load. Read it once, before the client work, not after.

How the categories tend to break down

  • Permissive (Apache‑2.0, MIT and similar). Commercial use is straightforward; attribution requirements are usually light. Much of the language, translation and vision stack falls here.
  • Open community licences. Commercial use is allowed but conditional — often on revenue scale or on acceptable-use terms. Several image and video families work this way.
  • Research or non-commercial only. Fine for learning and personal work, not for paid output. Always worth checking before a model becomes part of your habit.

Native runtime components

The inference stacks themselves — llama.cpp, stable-diffusion.cpp, GGML and ONNX Runtime — are open source under permissive licences. On Windows, the NVIDIA CUDA and cuDNN runtime libraries are redistributed under NVIDIA's own terms and are not covered by ours. The full third-party notice list ships with the app.

Your work

We claim nothing in what you make. Nothing is uploaded, so nothing is collected, and there is no clause anywhere granting us a licence to your output. What you generate is a file on your disk.
